Introduction

This report replicates key analyses from Ramirez et al., “Carbon pathways and trophic attributes are conserved in carnivorous reef fishes across a major human disturbance gradient,” which investigates how human disturbance influences energy flow and food web structure in a hyperdiverse coral reef ecosystem at Kiritimati Island. The original study leverages advanced stable isotope techniques, including compound-specific amino acid analysis, to evaluate whether shifts in habitat quality and community composition translate into changes in trophic structure and carbon sourcing for reef fishes.

The dataset is based on ecosystem-scale sampling across a well-defined gradient of human disturbance. It includes tissue samples from six carnivorous, generalist reef fish species that collectively represent a large proportion of reef fish biomass. For each sample, both bulk stable isotope values and essential amino acid isotope signatures were measured. These data are paired with isotopic signatures from multiple basal carbon sources, including plankton, detritus, coral-derived material, and epilithic algal matrices, enabling detailed comparison of energy pathways across sites with differing levels of disturbance.

To evaluate trophic structure and energy sourcing, the original study applied a combination of statistical and isotope-based methods. These included bulk isotope analysis to estimate trophic position, compound-specific isotope analysis to resolve carbon sources, and Bayesian mixing models to quantify source contributions. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was used to differentiate isotopic fingerprints among sources, while general linear models (GLMs) tested for variation in trophic metrics across the disturbance gradient. Isotopic niche analyses further assessed changes in niche width and position among species.

Despite pronounced differences in reef condition and community composition, the original study found that dominant energy pathways remained largely unchanged. Reef fishes consistently relied on planktonic production and microbially reworked detritus as primary energy sources, with minimal contributions from coral- and algae-based pathways. Trophic positions and niche characteristics were similarly stable across the disturbance gradient. These findings suggest that key energy channels in this system are relatively robust to human disturbance, potentially due to the stabilizing role of generalist consumers and persistent pelagic and detrital inputs.

This replication focuses on reproducing several core components of the original analysis. First, I will calculate and compare summary statistics of relative biomass and abundance, expressed as percentages of the total, across the six fish species and three levels of human disturbance. Second, I will recreate visualizations of essential amino acid isotope values, specifically phenylalanine (Phe) and lysine (Lys), across disturbance levels and sites for each species. Third, I will replicate the inferential component of the study by conducting a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to evaluate separation among carbon sources based on isotopic signatures. Finally, I will reproduce visualizations of the LDA results to assess how well isotopic fingerprints distinguish among sources and to compare patterns with those reported in the original study.

Disscussion & Reflection

Overall, the replication of analyses from Ramirez et al. was largely successful. The majority of summary statistics, visualizations, and inferential results closely matched those presented in the original paper, indicating that the main patterns and conclusions are reproducible using the provided data. In particular, comparisons of relative biomass and abundance across disturbance levels, as well as visualizations of essential amino acid values, were consistent with the published results. The linear discriminant analysis (LDA) and its associated plots also showed similar separation among carbon sources, suggesting that the key structure of the data was effectively reproduced.

While the replication was largely successful, there were some challenges encountered throughout the process. At times, it was not entirely clear how certain variables were processed or how specific figures were generated in the original study. This made it difficult to determine whether discrepancies were due to coding differences or underlying data handling steps that were not explicitly described. In a few cases, brief reference to the original code helped clarify these issues, but the majority of the replication was completed independently. These challenges highlight the importance of clear methodological descriptions and detailed reporting of data processing steps in published studies.

Some minor differences between the replicated results and the original outputs likely stem from differences in coding approaches and implementation. My analyses relied heavily on packages such as dplyr for data manipulation and summarization, whereas the original study primarily used base R functions. Although both approaches are valid, small differences in grouping behavior, ordering of factors, or handling of missing values can lead to slight variations in results or figure appearance. Despite these minor inconsistencies, the overall agreement between the replicated and original analyses suggests that the results are robust and that the study’s conclusions are well supported.
